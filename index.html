Method, assumptions & limits

This is a 1D thermal-resistance and pressure-drop model for concept sizing. Expect roughly ±20–30 % compared with CFD or test data. Validate final designs by simulation or measurement.

  • Heat transfer. Laminar flow uses Shah & London fully developed NuH1 and f·Re for the channel aspect ratio, plus a Hausen-type thermal-entry correction per straight pass. Turbulent flow uses Gnielinski with a short-duct correction. Between Re 2300 and 4000 the two are blended linearly. Bends are assumed to restart the boundary layer, and Dean-vortex enhancement is ignored, which is conservative.
  • 2D plate model. The plate face is divided into a grid of up to about 6,000 cells. Heat conducts in-plane through the local solid thickness. Cells above channels and headers are coupled to the coolant through the heated-face wall and the convective film. The coolant is marched along each channel path, and the plate and coolant are solved alternately until they agree. This model gives the map, the peak temperature and the component temperatures. Each component adds a TIM layer (t/(k·A)) and its junction-to-case resistance. Spreading through the plate thickness under small components is not modelled, so very small, intense sources read slightly optimistic.
  • Pump curve. With a pump curve, the flow is found by bisection where the pump pressure rise equals the plate pressure drop plus the rest-of-loop drop. The loop drop scales with flow². The pump curve is interpolated linearly between your points, and all results use the operating-point flow.
  • 1D model. Used for the pressure drop, heat-transfer coefficient and resistance breakdown, and as a quick cross-check.
  • Plate conduction (1D). The walls between channels act as fins. Heat is conducted through the heated-face wall and spreads laterally between channels and at the plate edges. Lateral conduction along the plate blends the result between two limits. The uniform heat flux limit (hottest point at the outlet) is conservative. The isothermal plate limit (ε-NTU) is ideal.
  • Pressure drop. Friction uses Shah's apparent friction factor (laminar, developing) and Haaland's (turbulent). 180° bends use the Darby 3-K method. Fixed loss coefficients cover channel entry and exit (0.5 + 1.0), headers (1.0 each, at header velocity) and ports (1.5 each, at port velocity).
  • Flow distribution. For parallel layouts, the inlet and outlet headers are split into segments between channel junctions. Each segment has friction plus a momentum term: the inlet header regains 1.0× the drop in velocity head, and the outlet header loses 2.0× the rise. Each channel follows its own pressure-drop curve (developing friction plus entry/exit losses). The solver iterates until the channel flows add up to the total. The per-channel flows set each channel's heat-transfer coefficient and coolant temperature rise in the 2D model, and the header losses in the pressure drop.
  • Fluids. Properties are evaluated at the mean bulk temperature. Glycol data are for concentrations by volume and are approximate (±5–10 %). Gases are treated as ideal and incompressible within the plate, with properties at the inlet pressure.
  • Not modelled. Contact and interface resistance (TIM), discrete heat sources and hot spots, radiation and natural convection, two-phase flow, turbulators and pin fins.
